# Concept Selection

Use this method only for the current research question. Reuse the existing brief, applicable user answers and private records; several selected methods contribute to one investigation, not repeated interviews. Inspect permitted actual evidence and preserve source links. Do not invent participants, quotations, emotions, findings, measurements or approvals; mark hypotheses and missing data explicitly. Planning a study does not authorise recruitment, messages, recording, tracking installation, spending, account changes or product changes. The current task and host permissions govern; shared automatic records are not proof that a study ran.
## What You Do
You run the decision that ends a parallel exploration. You fix the criteria before the options are compared, apply them to every concept, choose one, and record why the others lost. Use the existing decision source or requested comparison, not a new mandatory decision record or scoreboard — the reasoning is the part that survives the meeting.
## Criteria Before Comparison
Order matters more than the criteria themselves. Write down what would make a concept win before you look at the set. Criteria written afterwards describe the option you already preferred, with a scoring table on top.
Criteria come from the brief's success criteria and the product's principles, not from the room. Each one has to be capable of failing a concept:
| Weak criterion | Why it fails | Stronger form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Feels modern" | No concept can lose on it | "Uses only patterns already in the design system" |
| "Better UX" | Restates the goal | "Meets the task’s agreed effort and error requirements" |
| "Scalable" | Unfalsifiable at this stage | "Remains usable at the required content volume" |
Mark each criterion as a **threshold** (fail it and the concept is out) or a **trade-off** (weighed against the others). Mixing the two silently is how a concept that breaks a hard constraint stays in the conversation.
## Deciding Honestly
- **Evidence over volume.** A concept dies on a test result, a constraint, or a stated criterion — not on how many people in the room disliked it.
- **Name what the winner costs.** Every choice gives something up. A selection that reports no downside has not been examined; state what the winning concept sacrificed and what would make you revisit it.
- **A split set is a priority problem, not a design problem.** If two concepts each win on a different criterion, the criteria conflict and the team has a priority to settle. Escalate that rather than averaging the two into a compromise that leads on nothing.
- **Do not combine concepts blindly.** A user may deliberately choose a hybrid; explain interactions and test the combined result where needed rather than banning an informed mixed choice.
## The Rejected Concepts Are Half the Output
For each concept not chosen, retain useful context: what it tested, why it was not chosen and what evidence could justify revisiting it. This is the highest-value part of the record. It stops the team relitigating a settled direction six months later, and it feeds the existing design rationale when the decision has to be defended in writing.
## Best Practices
- Name who decides before the review — a selection with no owner defaults to the loudest voice in the room
- Apply the criteria to the incumbent too; the current design does not get a bye for arriving first
- Keep rejected concepts retrievable rather than deleted — revisiting is only cheap while the work still exists
- Do not select across mismatched fidelities; re-level the set first or the polish decides for you
- A production experiment can add evidence when traffic, ethics and authority permit; it neither replaces the user’s product choice nor makes every measurable change require an A/B test
