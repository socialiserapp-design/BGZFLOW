# Qual-Quant Triangulation

Use this method only for the current research question. Reuse the existing brief, applicable user answers and private records; several selected methods contribute to one investigation, not repeated interviews. Inspect permitted actual evidence and preserve source links. Do not invent participants, quotations, emotions, findings, measurements or approvals; mark hypotheses and missing data explicitly. Planning a study does not authorise recruitment, messages, recording, tracking installation, spending, account changes or product changes. The current task and host permissions govern; shared automatic records are not proof that a study ran.
## What You Do
You take two accounts of the same behaviour — one measured, one reported — and work out what their disagreement means, which one answers the question actually being asked, and what the next study has to look like to settle it. The output is a decision about what to believe and one study design, not a summary of both sources.
## Disagreement Is Information
Teams treat a conflict as a problem with one source. It can suggest where to investigate; the patterns below are hypotheses rather than established causes:
| What you see | What it usually means |
| --- | --- |
| Data shows abandonment; users report no difficulty | Possible price/timing effects, a decision made before arrival, unrepresented users or a tracking issue |
| Users report a serious problem; data shows no effect | Possible segment concentration, an unmeasured step, an insensitive metric or a research-sampling issue |
| Data improved; users report it feels worse | Possible proxy optimisation or a different affected population; check the actual downstream outcome |
| Users are enthusiastic; retention is flat | Reported intent may not predict return behaviour; also check discoverability, access and measurement |
| Both look fine; the business outcome does not | Possible mismatch between the metric and intended goal; also inspect data quality and missing segments |
The last two are the expensive ones, because nothing looks wrong until much later.
## Which Source Answers Which Question
Give each question to the source that can actually answer it, and stop asking the other:
- **What happened, how often, and where** — behavioural data. Interviews are a poor census; people misremember frequency badly.
- **Why, and what they were trying to do** — research. No amount of event data recovers intent.
- **Whether the thing is worth building at all** — neither, on its own. That is a judgment, and dressing it as a finding is how teams launder a decision they already made.
When someone asks a why-question of a dashboard, or a how-many-question of six interviews, the two sources may answer different questions. Resolve the category mismatch without assuming either underlying account is correct.
## Designing the Study That Settles It
A resolving study is narrower than either original. Write down, before running it: the specific claim in dispute, what result would make you drop the qualitative account, and what result would make you drop the quantitative one. If no result could change your mind, you are not resolving the conflict, you are building a case.
Prefer the cheap instrument that discriminates. An already authorised, relevant session observation may distinguish hypotheses more cheaply than another broad study; do not start recording or contact participants merely because the method suggests it. If the dispute is about *why*, add measurement to the qualitative session rather than running two studies.
## Best Practices
- Name which source is load-bearing for the decision before you look at either
- Check whether the observed behaviour and reported preference answer the same question and describe the same population. Prefer the evidence relevant to that claim; neither is automatically correct
- Check that both sources describe the same population before calling it a contradiction — different segments are not a disagreement
- Do not average the two accounts into a compromise finding; a middle position neither source supports is worse than picking one
- Do not resolve a conflict by re-running the study that produced the answer you prefer
